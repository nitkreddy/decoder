The Case for Small Modular Nuclear Reactors

November 22, 2026 · 7 Minute Read

One of the more interesting things about the growth of artificial intelligence is that some of its biggest constraints might not have much to do with artificial intelligence at all. Companies can keep designing better chips, building larger data centers, and developing more capable models, but all of that infrastructure needs somewhere to get its electricity.

This is part of the reason nuclear energy is becoming interesting again. But what I find particularly compelling is not necessarily the prospect of building more traditional nuclear power plants. It is the possibility of changing how nuclear power plants themselves are built and deployed.

Nuclear Energy Has a Construction Problem

Nuclear fission is not a particularly new technology. We have been using it to generate electricity commercially for decades, and the United States already operates approximately 98 GW of nuclear generating capacity [1]. We know that nuclear power can work at scale. The problem is that building new nuclear infrastructure has historically been pretty difficult.

A conventional nuclear power plant requires a huge amount of upfront capital, specialized labor, regulatory approval, and years of construction before it starts producing electricity. A delay of even a couple of years can have a pretty substantial impact on the financial viability of the entire project.

There is also a difference between getting better at designing nuclear reactors and getting better at building them. If every new plant is treated as a unique infrastructure project, some of the lessons learned during construction might not carry over particularly well to the next one.

That is where small modular reactors, or SMRs, become interesting.

What Makes a Small Modular Reactor Different?

A conventional nuclear reactor typically has an electrical generating capacity somewhere between 550 MW and 1,500 MW. Small modular reactors generally produce around 300 MW or less per unit [1]. But the most interesting part of an SMR is not necessarily its size. It is the modularity.

The idea is that a greater portion of the reactor can be manufactured in a controlled factory environment, transported to its destination, and assembled on-site. Instead of building a completely different reactor every time a customer needs one, manufacturers could produce multiple units based on the same design.

This introduces some of the advantages we see in other manufacturing industries. Engineers can refine production processes, workers can get better at assembling the same components, and suppliers can invest in equipment knowing that there might be demand for more than one unit.

Of course, a nuclear power plant is not a car. There are still site-specific engineering requirements, regulatory considerations, and a substantial amount of infrastructure that needs to be built on-site. Factory fabrication does not eliminate those problems. It just creates an opportunity to reduce some of the work that has to be repeated at every site.

Why AI Companies Care About Nuclear Power

Data centers are a particularly interesting potential customer for nuclear energy because their electricity requirements are large and relatively continuous. A company running thousands of GPUs does not necessarily care whether the sun is shining or the wind is blowing. It needs electricity whenever those GPUs are being used.

To put the scale into perspective, consider a hypothetical data center that draws 200 MW continuously. Over the course of a year, that facility would consume approximately 1.75 TWh of electricity. A 300 MW nuclear reactor operating at a 90% capacity factor would generate approximately 2.37 TWh over that same period.

That does not mean one reactor can automatically support the entire data center. Both facilities would need to account for maintenance, outages, transmission losses, and changes in demand. Annual electricity generation and the ability to provide electricity at any given moment are two different things.

But it does illustrate why smaller nuclear reactors could be appealing. A customer might not need an entire gigawatt-scale power plant. It might just need a few hundred megawatts of reliable generating capacity, with the option to add more as its operations expand.

And AI is only one potential application. Industrial manufacturing, chemical processing, desalination, and remote communities could also benefit from smaller, more flexible sources of energy. Some advanced reactor designs could even supply heat directly to industrial processes rather than converting all of their thermal energy into electricity.

There Is a Trade-Off to Making Reactors Smaller

One thing worth pointing out is that making a nuclear reactor smaller does not necessarily make the electricity it produces cheaper. There is a good reason conventional reactors were designed to be so large in the first place.

A larger reactor can spread certain fixed costs across more electricity generation. Security, staffing, regulatory compliance, and supporting infrastructure do not necessarily scale linearly with reactor output. A reactor that produces one-third as much electricity might still require more than one-third of the supporting infrastructure.

The argument for SMRs is that some of those disadvantages could be offset by standardized designs, simpler construction, and repeated manufacturing [2]. Multiple reactors at the same site could also share certain systems and personnel.

But to actually benefit from manufacturing at scale, companies need to manufacture at scale. Building one small modular reactor is not enough to demonstrate the advantages of building fifty of them.

This creates a pretty difficult problem for the industry. Customers want evidence that these reactors can be built affordably before placing large orders, but manufacturers need those large orders to bring down the cost of building them.

What Is Actually Being Built?

One of the projects I find particularly interesting is Ontario Power Generation's Darlington New Nuclear Project in Canada. The project involves four BWRX-300 reactors, each designed to generate 300 MW of electricity. Construction began in 2025, with the first reactor expected to begin generating electricity in 2030, according to reporting from August 2026 [3].

What makes this project interesting is that it is not just a demonstration of a single reactor. The plan is to build four units at the same site, which creates an opportunity to see whether the experience gained from building the first reactor actually translates into improvements when constructing subsequent units.

That is a much more useful test of the modular nuclear thesis than simply getting one reactor operational. If the second, third, and fourth reactors can be delivered with fewer delays and less construction work, it would provide some real evidence that standardized deployment has value.

There is also quite a bit of variety within the advanced nuclear industry itself. Companies are pursuing different reactor sizes, coolants, fuels, and operating temperatures. Some designs build on the conventional water-cooled reactors that the industry already has experience with, while others introduce substantially different technologies [4].

These are not necessarily interchangeable approaches. A reactor designed to provide electricity to a utility might look very different from one designed to supply high-temperature heat to an industrial customer.

The Bottleneck Might Not Be Reactor Technology

I think one of the more overlooked aspects of advanced nuclear is the infrastructure around the reactor itself. A company could develop a technically impressive reactor design and still struggle to commercialize it because it cannot secure the necessary fuel, find qualified suppliers, or navigate the construction and licensing process.

Some advanced reactor designs also require fuel that is different from what most existing commercial nuclear plants use. Expanding the production and availability of those fuels is a separate challenge from designing the reactors that consume them.

There is also the issue of timing. Electricity demand from new data centers could grow much faster than nuclear power plants can be licensed and constructed. A customer that needs electricity in two years might not have the option of waiting for a reactor that will be ready in six.

This is one reason I do not think the growth of nuclear energy necessarily comes at the expense of renewable energy, natural gas, or battery storage. Different technologies solve different problems, and in some cases the best solution might involve a combination of them.

Nuclear energy does not need to be the best solution for every electricity customer to become a much bigger industry. It just needs to be a compelling solution for enough of them.

Where the Value Could Accrue

There are a few different ways companies could benefit from the growth of advanced nuclear energy. Reactor developers are the most obvious ones, but they are also taking on substantial technical, regulatory, and financial risk.

Fuel suppliers, specialized component manufacturers, and nuclear-qualified engineering firms could benefit from the deployment of multiple reactor designs. A company supplying critical components to several reactor manufacturers might not need to correctly predict which specific reactor architecture ends up winning commercially.

There could also be opportunities in industrial software. Nuclear facilities require extensive monitoring, inspection, maintenance planning, and documentation. As more facilities are deployed, software that reduces the cost and complexity of those activities could become increasingly valuable, provided it meets the industry's safety and validation requirements.

One of the things I find interesting about frontier technologies in general is that the company building the most recognizable end product is not necessarily the only company capturing meaningful value. Sometimes the more compelling business is the one supplying something that every competing manufacturer needs.

What I Would Watch Over the Next Few Years

There are three things I would pay particular attention to as the SMR industry develops.

First, I would look at how construction costs and timelines change between successive reactors of the same design. If every deployment takes just as long as the first one, some of the advantages of modularity might not materialize.

Second, I would look at the nature of customer commitments. An agreement to explore the possibility of purchasing nuclear power is very different from a binding contract with financing and a realistic path to construction.

Third, I would watch the development of the supporting supply chain. If specialized components, fuel, or qualified labor remain difficult to obtain, having more customers interested in nuclear energy will not necessarily translate into more reactors being built.

There are also some pretty obvious risks. Construction delays, financing costs, public opposition, waste management, and competing energy technologies could all limit adoption. And it is possible that only a handful of the reactor designs being developed today will ever be deployed commercially at meaningful scale.

The milestone I would be most excited about is not necessarily the first commercial deployment of a new reactor design. It is the point at which building the next one becomes a relatively predictable process.

Conclusion

Small modular reactors are interesting because they represent a different approach to deploying a technology that we already know can generate useful amounts of electricity. The opportunity is not just to make nuclear reactors smaller. It is to make nuclear infrastructure more standardized, repeatable, and accessible to a wider range of customers.

Whether the industry can actually accomplish that is still an open question. There is a pretty big difference between designing a reactor that can theoretically be manufactured at scale and building the industrial infrastructure necessary to manufacture it at scale.

But if companies can make that transition, nuclear energy could start to look a little less like a series of enormous infrastructure projects and a little more like a manufacturing industry. And I think that is a much more interesting development than simply making a nuclear reactor smaller.
